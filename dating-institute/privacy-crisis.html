Your Data on Dating Apps

What dating apps collect, who they share it with, and the scandals they hoped you'd forget.

88% fail basic privacy standards

Mozilla's Privacy Not Included review evaluated the most popular dating apps in 2024. The result: 88% of dating apps failed basic privacy criteria. Not some obscure test — basic privacy. The apps that millions trust with their most intimate data are overwhelmingly failing to protect it.

Dating apps collect an average of 16 data points per user, including location, photos, messages, political views, health history, sexual preferences, and biometric data. This isn't metadata — it's the most personal information you could possibly give an app.

What they collect

When researchers examined the top 15 dating apps, they found every single one leaked data. But the data they collect in the first place is equally concerning:

  • Precise location — often tracked continuously, not just when the app is open
  • Biometric data — facial recognition from your photos (Bumble was fined for this)
  • Sexual orientation and preferences — shared with third-party advertisers
  • Health information — including HIV status (Grindr shared this)
  • Political views — collected through profile prompts, shared with data brokers
  • Message content — your private conversations, stored and analyzed
  • Behavioral patterns — who you swipe on, how long you look, when you're active

The real cost of “free” tiers

Most dating apps offer a free tier. But free doesn't mean they aren't making money from you. If you're not paying for the product, you ARE the product. Your data is sold to advertisers, data brokers, and third parties. Your behavioral patterns are used to train algorithms that optimize for engagement (not for your wellbeing).

The irony: users who pay $50/mo for premium still have their data collected and shared. Paying doesn't buy privacy — it just buys features.
