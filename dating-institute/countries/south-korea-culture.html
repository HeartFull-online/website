Dating Culture in South Korea

South Korean dating culture is one of the most distinctive in the world. It glorifies coupledom with a fervor that produces matching outfits, 100-day anniversary celebrations, and a complex vocabulary for every stage of a relationship. At the same time, a hyper-competitive society has created a generation that increasingly opts out of dating entirely. The result is a culture that simultaneously celebrates romance and makes it harder to achieve than ever.

How people traditionally meet

Before dating apps, the most common way to meet a partner in South Korea was through sogaeting (blind dates arranged by mutual friends). A typical sogaeting works like this: two friends each bring a single friend, and the four meet at a cafe. The blind date pair exchange polite conversation, and afterward decide whether to meet again. The atmosphere is formal yet friendly, and sogaeting remains popular today as a trusted, socially acceptable path to meeting someone new.

Other traditional meeting channels include group dates (similar to Japanese goukon), university clubs, church groups, and workplace introductions. For older or more marriage-minded individuals, seon (formal arranged dates, often set up by parents) serve a similar function to Japanese omiai. In a seon, families may exchange background information before the couple meets.

The "some" stage

Korean dating has a unique pre-relationship phase called "some" (from the English "something"). When two people are in the "some" stage, they are clearly interested in each other — texting constantly, going on dates — but have not yet formally confessed their feelings through gobaek (the Korean equivalent of Japan's kokuhaku). The "some" stage can last weeks or months and is a recognized, named phase of Korean courtship. It ends when one person makes a formal confession and the other accepts.

This creates a clear boundary: you are either in the "some" stage, officially dating, or nothing at all. The ambiguity that Americans experience around exclusivity does not exist in the same way, because the gobaek moment defines the relationship's start.

Couple culture: matching outfits and milestone celebrations

Once officially together, Korean couples engage in rituals that would seem unusual elsewhere. Couple look (matching or coordinated outfits) is widespread — from identical t-shirts to complementary color schemes, couples use fashion to publicly signal their relationship status. In contrast to Western norms where matching outfits might seem excessive, in Korea it is a celebrated expression of unity and affection.

Korean couples celebrate relationship milestones with an intensity uncommon in other cultures. The 100-day anniversary (baegil) is the first major milestone, rooted in the traditional celebration of a baby's 100th day of life. Couples exchange rings, plan special dates, and give gifts. Beyond 100 days, couples celebrate at 200, 300, 500, and 1,000 days. There are also couple-specific holidays: every 14th of the month has a designated theme, from Rose Day (May 14) to Hug Day (December 14).

Date culture: cafes, PC bangs, and photo booths

Korean date culture revolves around themed cafes (cat cafes, board game cafes, study cafes), PC bangs (internet gaming rooms where couples play games together), photo booth sessions (collecting strips of couple photos is a relationship ritual), and elaborate food dates. Korean couples tend to go on dates frequently — multiple times per week is normal — and the variety of date types reflects the creativity embedded in Korean couple culture.

Academic pressure and delayed dating

South Korea's intense academic and professional competition has a direct impact on dating. Students spend years preparing for the CSAT (college entrance exam), followed by intense university study and the hyper-competitive job market. Many young Koreans report that they simply have no time or energy for dating, as they pour resources into acquiring qualifications, language skills, and professional experience.

This has contributed to a dramatic demographic shift. In 1970, only 1.4% of women aged 30-34 had never married. By 2010, that figure reached nearly 30%. The number of singles has increased 20-fold since 1970. Marriage intentions continue to decline, with growing ambivalence toward marriage among young adults of both genders.

Who pays and gender dynamics

Traditional Korean dating norms expect the man to pay, especially on early dates and particularly at restaurants. However, couples often develop a rhythm where the man pays for meals and the woman covers dessert, coffee, or movie tickets. Among younger, more progressive couples, splitting bills evenly is becoming more accepted, though it is still less common than in Western countries.

Family approval

Family plays a significant but carefully timed role in Korean relationships. Unlike some Asian cultures where family involvement begins early, Korean couples typically delay introducing partners to parents until the relationship is serious and marriage is under discussion. When the meeting does happen, it carries weight: parental disapproval can end otherwise strong relationships. Factors like education, family background, career prospects, and even blood type are considered.

Online vs. offline dating

Dating apps have gained traction in South Korea, but Gen Z Koreans are increasingly rejecting the swiping mentality in favor of slower, more deliberate connection. Apps like Amanda, Noondate, and Glam are popular domestically, often with verification features that appeal to Korea's status-conscious dating culture. However, traditional methods — sogaeting, group dates, and introductions through friends — remain deeply embedded in how people meet.

The rise of honbap culture (eating alone without stigma) and broader acceptance of independent lifestyles has given young Koreans an alternative identity that does not center on romantic partnership. Choosing to be single has become a legitimate lifestyle, not a failure state — a dramatic shift from the intense couple culture that still dominates Korean media and public life.

Taboos and cultural sensitivities

Public displays of affection are more accepted in Korea than in Japan, but heavy PDA (kissing, prolonged embracing) in public is still frowned upon by older generations. Age hierarchy matters — the older partner is typically addressed with honorifics even in romantic relationships. Discussing past relationships in detail is considered inappropriate. And while Korean culture celebrates coupledom intensely, there remains a strong social stigma around being single past a certain age, particularly for women.
