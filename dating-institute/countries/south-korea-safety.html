Dating App Safety: South Korea

South Korea's dating app safety landscape has been shaped by crisis. The molka (hidden camera) epidemic and the Nth Room scandal forced sweeping legislative changes that make South Korea one of the most heavily regulated dating markets in the world. Real-name verification, strict digital sex crime laws, and government oversight define the current environment.

The molka crisis

Molka — the Korean term for hidden spy cameras — became a national crisis that directly impacts dating safety. Reports of molka crimes surged from roughly 1,100 in 2010 to 6,800 in 2018. Hidden cameras were discovered in hotel rooms, public restrooms, and changing rooms. For women, the fear of being secretly filmed transformed how they approach dating, particularly meeting strangers from apps at private locations.

In 2018, over 70,000 South Korean women marched in Seoul demanding stronger action, carrying signs reading "My life is not your porn." The protests were among the largest women's rights demonstrations in the country's history and accelerated legislative reform.

The Nth Room scandal

In March 2020, the arrest of Cho Ju-bin exposed the "Nth Room" — a network of secret Telegram chatrooms where women and underage girls were blackmailed into producing sexually exploitative content for paying members. At least 74 victims were identified, including 16 minors. The case shocked the nation and triggered fundamental changes to how South Korea regulates digital platforms.

In direct response, the National Assembly strengthened digital sex crime laws, making it a crime to possess, buy, store, or view non-consensually captured images, with penalties of up to three years in prison or fines of 30 million won. The age of consent was raised from 13 to 16. These reforms apply broadly to any platform where sexual exploitation can occur, including dating apps.

Real-name verification

South Korea uses a national real-name verification system tied to resident registration numbers. Online services, including dating apps, can verify users' identities through mobile carrier data. This system makes it significantly harder to create fake profiles compared to markets like the US, where anonymous sign-up is the norm. However, the system is not without flaws — a dating app operator was fined in 2024 for creating fake accounts using real users' photos without consent.

Government oversight (KISA)

The Korea Internet & Security Agency (KISA) oversees digital platform compliance, including dating apps. In 2018, the Korean Women's Human Rights Promotion Agency opened the Digital Sexual Crime Victim Support Center under the Ministry of Gender Equality and Family, providing counseling, deletion of illicit material, and investigative and litigation support for victims. This infrastructure represents one of the most comprehensive government responses to digital dating safety anywhere in the world.

How safe do users feel?

Despite strong regulations, safety concerns remain elevated — particularly among women. The molka crisis created lasting anxiety about meeting strangers, and deepfake pornography has emerged as the latest threat. A 2022 survey found that South Korean women are more likely than their counterparts in any other developed nation to take extensive safety precautions before app-arranged dates, including sharing location with friends and researching their match's identity beforehand.

Tips for dating app users in South Korea

Use verified platforms. Choose apps that require real-name verification via mobile carrier. Check for hidden cameras — small detection devices are widely sold and used. Meet in well-lit public places for first dates. Never share intimate images through any digital channel. Report crimes to the Digital Sexual Crime Victim Support Center (02-735-8994) or local police.
