Japan: Where Adult Diapers Outsell Baby Diapers

Japan is a country where the future arrived early — and it looks old. With 29% of the population over 65, adult diapers have outsold baby diapers every year since 2011. In 2024, only 686,061 babies were born, the lowest figure since records began in 1899. For every new baby, more than two people died.

The fertility decline

Japan's total fertility rate has been below the 2.1 replacement level since the mid-1970s. But recent years have accelerated the decline. From 1.34 in 2007 to 1.15 in 2024, the trend shows no sign of reversing. The 2024 birth count of 686,061 was a 5.7% drop from the previous year — the ninth consecutive annual decline.

Meanwhile, deaths reached a postwar record of 1,605,298 in 2024. The natural population decrease — the gap between births and deaths — hit 919,237, also a record. Japan's population peaked at 128.5 million in 2010 and has fallen to roughly 123.4 million as of 2025. Demographers project it will drop below 100 million by 2050.

The herbivore men phenomenon

In Japanese media, the term soshoku danshi (“herbivore men”) describes young men who have little interest in pursuing romantic or sexual relationships. The phenomenon has been debated since the late 2000s, but the data behind it is real.

A University of Tokyo study analyzing national surveys from 1987 to 2015 found that the share of single men aged 18–39 grew from 40.4% to 50.8%. For women, the figure rose from 27.4% to 40.7%. Half of these singles reported no interest in romantic relationships whatsoever.

“In 2015, around one in four women and one in three men in their thirties were unmarried and not in a heterosexual relationship. Half of the singles reported no interest in romantic relationships.”

The causes are structural, not just cultural. Lower income, precarious employment, and the extreme demands of Japan’s corporate work culture all contribute. The phenomenon is not that young people have suddenly become asexual — it is that the conditions for forming relationships have become prohibitively difficult.

Overwork culture and karoshi

Japan coined the word karoshi — literally “death by overwork” — because the phenomenon needed a name. A government survey found that one in ten Japanese workers logs more than 80 hours of overtime per month, with one in five at risk of karoshi through stroke, heart attack, or stress-induced suicide.

The connection to fertility is direct. When people work 60–80 hour weeks, there is no time or energy left for dating, courtship, or raising children. Japan’s work culture creates a vicious cycle: as the working-age population shrinks due to low births, the remaining workers shoulder heavier loads, further discouraging family formation.

In Japan, nearly all births occur within marriage — out-of-wedlock births account for only about 2–3% of the total, compared to 40% in the US. This means the marriage rate is effectively a ceiling on the birth rate. And with marriages hitting a historically low 505,656 in 2024, the birth rate has nowhere to go but down.

The dating app paradox

Japan has a $271 million dating app market, yet dating apps account for only 0.08% of all app downloads in the country — the lowest proportion among major economies. Japanese culture still carries a stigma around online dating, viewing it as a last resort rather than a normal way to meet people.

The dominant dating app is not Tinder but Pairs, a domestic app launched in 2012 by Eureka (now owned by Match Group). Pairs generated over $77 million in app revenue in Japan in 2023, making it the country's top-grossing dating app. The company claims over 20 million registered users and estimates that Pairs accounts for roughly 10% of marriages in Japan.

While Tinder leads in raw downloads (1.28 million in 2023), Pairs dominates revenue because it targets serious relationship-seekers with a subscription model that charges for meaningful features. Other popular Japanese dating apps include Tapple, with, and Omiai — all designed with a relationship-first rather than hookup-first approach.

The government response

Japan’s government has thrown nearly everything at the problem. Municipalities run state-sponsored matchmaking events (konkatsu, or “marriage hunting” activities). Subsidies for childcare, housing, and parental leave have been expanded repeatedly. In 2026, a regional government began offering residents ¥20,000 ($125) subsidies for using dating apps — attempting to intervene upstream of baby-making, at the relationship-formation stage itself.

The government has also set up AI-powered matchmaking systems in multiple prefectures, using algorithms to suggest compatible partners to residents who register voluntarily. Tokyo launched a government-run dating app in 2024. These programs reflect an uncomfortable reality: the private dating app market has failed to reverse — or even slow — the decline.

Despite decades of pro-natalist policy, nothing has worked. The fertility rate continues to drop. The programs address symptoms (cost of childcare, lack of meeting opportunities) without touching the deeper structural issues: overwork, economic precarity among young people, and a dating app industry that monetizes loneliness rather than resolving it.

The diaper metric

The fact that adult diapers have outsold baby diapers in Japan since 2011 is more than a striking headline — it is a demographic verdict. The adult diaper market is forecast to grow 16% over the five years to 2027, reaching over $600 million. Baby diaper sales are projected to shrink 8%, to around $530 million. Manufacturer Oji Holdings, once a baby diaper company, has pivoted its production lines entirely toward the adult market.

When a society's fastest-growing consumer segment is incontinence products for the elderly, the future is already here.

What Japan tells us

Japan is the world's most advanced case study in demographic decline. It shows what happens when:

  1. Work culture makes relationships structurally difficult
  2. Cultural norms tie births almost exclusively to marriage
  3. Young people rationally opt out of a system that offers them exhaustion
  4. Dating apps capture revenue without generating relationships at scale
  5. Government intervention comes too late and too shallow

The dating app industry in Japan — worth $271 million — has not reversed any of these trends. Pairs may account for 10% of marriages, but marriages themselves keep falling. The apps are a band-aid on a structural wound. And with only 0.08% of app downloads going to dating apps, most Japanese people are not even trying the band-aid.

Japan is where every other developed nation is heading. The question is not whether other countries will face similar dynamics, but when.
