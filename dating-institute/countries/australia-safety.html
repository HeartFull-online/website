Dating App Safety: Australia

Australia has built the most comprehensive government oversight structure for dating app safety in the world. The eSafety Commissioner — a role with no equivalent in the US or Europe — has direct regulatory authority over online platforms, backed by the Online Safety Act 2021. Combined with the ACCC's aggressive consumer protection enforcement and romance scam losses exceeding $139 million per year, Australia offers a case study in what active government intervention looks like.

The eSafety Commissioner

Australia's eSafety Commissioner is unique globally — a dedicated government official with statutory powers to regulate online safety, including on dating platforms. The Commissioner can issue industry codes, set standards for content moderation, and compel platforms to take action against harmful content. No other country has created a comparable role with this level of authority specifically focused on online safety.

Under the Online Safety Act 2021, the eSafety Commissioner can issue removal notices for intimate images shared without consent, require platforms to report on their safety practices, and take enforcement action against non-compliant services. The Commissioner has stated that self-declaration of age is insufficient for platform compliance, pushing dating apps toward more robust verification measures including photo ID, AI-based age estimation, and credit card checks.

Online Safety Act 2021

The Online Safety Act 2021 established the legal framework for regulating online platforms in Australia. Key provisions relevant to dating apps include the Online Content Scheme, which allows the eSafety Commissioner to issue takedown notices for harmful content, and the Basic Online Safety Expectations, which require platforms to take reasonable steps to ensure user safety.

A new safety code for dating apps has taken effect, requiring participating platforms to implement measures against harassment, fake profiles, and scam activity. While the code is voluntary and industry-led, the eSafety Commissioner retains the power to impose mandatory standards if the voluntary approach proves insufficient — a regulatory backstop that gives the voluntary code real teeth.

Romance scam losses

Romance scams are a massive problem in Australia. In 2025, Australians lost $139.9 million to romance scams, making it the third-highest scam category after investment scams ($837.7M) and payment redirection scams ($166.8M). Tinder, Bumble, and Facebook Dating are the most common starting points for dating-app-originated romance scams.

The National Anti-Scam Centre, established within the ACCC, created a dedicated Romance Scam Fusion Cell in 2025 that brought together dating platforms, social media companies, law enforcement, banks, cryptocurrency exchanges, and academic experts. The March 2026 fusion cell report documented how the coordinated crackdown disrupted scam networks and helped prevent millions in losses.

ACCC enforcement

The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) has been actively targeting dating platforms. The ACCC conducted a sweep of dating sites to identify misleading practices, published best-practice guidelines for dating platforms, and uses Scamwatch data to identify emerging threats. The ACCC's approach combines consumer protection enforcement with industry guidance — a carrot-and-stick model that has produced measurable results.

Total scam losses in Australia exceeded $2 billion in 2025, prompting calls for platforms to bear greater responsibility. The Scam Prevention Framework, currently being legislated, would impose obligations on banks, telcos, and online platforms to detect, prevent, and respond to scams — with significant penalties for failure.

Age verification debates

Australia has been at the forefront of age verification policy globally. The government introduced social media age restrictions and is pushing platforms toward robust verification methods. For dating apps, the eSafety Commissioner has mandated that simple self-declaration is not enough — platforms must use meaningful age assurance measures. The debate centers on balancing user privacy with child protection, a tension Australia is resolving by investing in privacy-preserving verification technologies rather than avoiding verification altogether.

How Australia differs from the US

The fundamental difference is institutional. The US has no eSafety Commissioner, no Online Safety Act, and no dedicated government body overseeing dating app safety. The FTC takes occasional enforcement action; Australia has built a permanent regulatory infrastructure. Australia's ACCC actively monitors and publishes data on dating scam losses; the US has no equivalent public reporting system. Australia's voluntary dating app safety code, backed by mandatory powers, represents a regulatory model the US has not attempted.

Australia also treats romance scams as a systemic problem requiring coordinated response, not just individual bad luck. The Fusion Cell model — bringing platforms, banks, law enforcement, and researchers together — is a fundamentally different approach from the US model of leaving consumers to report individual incidents.

Practical safety tips for Australia

Report scams to Scamwatch (scamwatch.gov.au) — your report feeds directly into the ACCC's anti-scam intelligence. Use the eSafety Commissioner's complaint process if intimate images are shared without consent. Be wary of matches who quickly move conversations off-platform. Never send money to someone you have not met in person, regardless of their story. Check the ACCC's dating site best-practice guidelines to evaluate whether your platform meets safety standards.
