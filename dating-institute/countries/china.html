China: The $1.5 Billion Dating Superpower

China is the world's second-largest dating app market at $1,497 million, commanding 22% of global share. It is also a demographic time bomb. The total fertility rate has collapsed from 1.67 in 2007 to 1.01 in 2024 — a 40% decline — and the government is now so alarmed that it builds its own dating apps and subsidizes matchmaking events.

The one-child legacy

No country's dating landscape is shaped more profoundly by government policy than China's. The one-child policy (1980–2015) created an entire generation of only children and a severe gender imbalance — at its peak, 117 boys were born for every 100 girls. Tens of millions of men are statistically unable to find a partner. The policy was relaxed to two children in 2016, then three in 2021, but the damage was already done.

The fertility rate barely responded. China's TFR was 1.67 in 2007, already below replacement. It hovered around 1.6–1.7 for a decade, then fell off a cliff after 2020: 1.28 in 2021, 1.09 in 2022, 1.05 in 2023, and 1.01 in 2024. This is one of the steepest fertility collapses in human history, exceeded only by South Korea.

The demographic crisis

In 2022, China's population declined for the first time in six decades. Deaths exceeded births by 850,000 people. In 2023, the gap widened to over 2 million. The UN now projects China's population will fall from 1.41 billion today to under 1 billion by 2075. This is not a slow decline — it is a structural collapse with no historical precedent for a country of this size.

The economic implications are staggering. China's working-age population is already shrinking. The old-age dependency ratio is climbing rapidly. The pension system, the real estate market (where young families are the primary buyers), and the manufacturing base all depend on births that are no longer happening.

"Leftover women" and 996 culture

Chinese dating culture operates under intense and contradictory pressures. Women who remain unmarried past their mid-twenties face the stigma of being called sheng nu — "leftover women" — a term popularized by the All-China Women's Federation in 2007. The pressure to marry early collides with the reality that educated urban women increasingly refuse to accept traditional gender roles.

Meanwhile, the 996 work culture (9am to 9pm, 6 days a week) leaves young professionals with almost no time or energy for dating. In cities like Beijing and Shanghai, sky-high housing prices mean a couple may need to save for a decade before affording a home — and home ownership is culturally expected before marriage. Many young Chinese have simply given up. The "lying flat" (tang ping) and "let it rot" (bai lan) movements reflect a generation that sees marriage and children as economically impossible.

Government intervention

What makes China unique is the government's direct involvement in matchmaking. Provincial governments have launched state-run dating apps, organized mass speed-dating events, and established subsidized matchmaking centers. Some municipalities offer cash bonuses for marriage and childbirth. The Communist Youth League operates dating platforms targeting young party members.

These efforts have been largely unsuccessful. Young Chinese are skeptical of government-run dating services, and the underlying economic pressures — housing costs, work hours, childcare costs — remain unaddressed. The government can build dating apps, but it cannot legislate desire. The fertility rate continues to fall despite every policy lever being pulled.

The Hello Group duopoly

China's commercial dating market is dominated by Hello Group (formerly Momo Inc.), which owns both Momo and Tantan — the country's two largest dating platforms. Momo, launched in 2011, has 66.7 million monthly active users and operates as a social+dating hybrid with livestreaming revenue. Tantan, often called "Chinese Tinder," was acquired by Hello Group in 2018 and has over 50 million users.

Unlike the US duopoly of Match Group and Bumble, Hello Group faces competition from Tencent-backed alternatives like Soul, which uses avatar-based profiles and virtual socializing. Marriage-focused platforms like Baihe (85 million registered users) and Zhenai cater to parents and families seeking arranged introductions. The market is segmented between casual/social apps and serious/marriage platforms in a way the Western market is not.

Why China matters

China represents the most dramatic test case for whether dating apps can coexist with demographic sustainability. The country has the world's largest population, the second-largest dating market, and a government actively trying to reverse fertility decline. If the combination of state intervention, commercial dating platforms, and pronatalist policy cannot stabilize China's birth rate, it suggests the problem is deeper than any app or policy can reach.

The Chinese experience also shows that the dating app business model may be structurally incompatible with its stated purpose. Hello Group generates the majority of its revenue from livestreaming and virtual gifts — entertainment features that keep users on the platform rather than off it. The incentive is engagement, not partnership. The same misalignment that plagues Western dating apps exists in China, wrapped in different cultural packaging.
