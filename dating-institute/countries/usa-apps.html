Market consolidation

The US dating app market looks competitive from the outside — dozens of apps, each with distinct branding. But the ownership structure tells a different story. Match Group (Dallas, TX) owns Tinder, Hinge, OkCupid, Match.com, POF, BLK, Chispa, and over 40 other brands. Bumble Inc. (Austin, TX) owns Bumble and Badoo. Together, these two companies control more than 70% of all US dating app revenue.

This duopoly means that switching from one app to another rarely means switching companies. A user frustrated with Tinder who moves to Hinge is still paying Match Group. Someone leaving Bumble for Badoo is still funding Bumble Inc. The illusion of choice masks a deeply consolidated market where competitive pressure to innovate on user outcomes is minimal.

Market dynamics

The US dating culture is defined by app-first meeting. As of 2025, dating apps are the #1 way American couples meet, surpassing friends, work, bars, and every other channel. Yet 78% of users report emotional exhaustion, and 80% of college students are skipping apps entirely. The market is enormous but increasingly distrusted.

Revenue growth has come not from user growth but from monetization escalation. Tinder introduced a $500/month "Select" tier in 2023. Hinge charges $50/month for "HingeX." The industry has learned that a shrinking, frustrated user base can still be squeezed for more per-user revenue — at least until it can't.

What this tells us

The US market is HeartFull's primary thesis case. When two companies control 70%+ of how a nation forms relationships, and those companies are financially incentivized to keep users paying rather than partnered, the system is structurally misaligned. The $3.5B in annual revenue is not a measure of success — it is a measure of how much money can be extracted from loneliness. HeartFull's model — disconnecting payments from connections — is designed to break exactly this dynamic.
