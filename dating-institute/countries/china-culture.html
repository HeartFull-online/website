Dating Culture in China

Chinese dating culture sits at the intersection of ancient tradition and modern crisis. Parents negotiate marriages in public parks while their adult children swipe through dating apps after 12-hour work shifts. A historic gender imbalance from the one-child policy, crushing economic pressure, and a government desperate to reverse declining birth rates create a dating landscape unlike any other on earth.

How people traditionally meet

Traditional Chinese matchmaking was a family affair. Marriages were arranged by parents, often with the help of a meiren (matchmaker) who evaluated compatibility based on family background, social status, and astrological alignment. While this formal system has declined in cities, its spirit persists. Parents remain deeply involved in their children's romantic lives, and family approval is not just desired but often considered essential.

In modern China, people meet through workplace connections, university classmates, and friend introductions. But the most distinctly Chinese meeting ground is the matchmaking park. In cities like Shanghai, Hangzhou, Shenzhen, and Tianjin, parents of unmarried adults gather every weekend in public parks, displaying their children's "resumes" on bulletin boards or open umbrellas. These listings include age, education, income, occupation, property ownership, and height. Parents walk through, compare profiles, exchange phone numbers, and negotiate potential matches — often without their children's knowledge or consent.

"Leftover women" and social pressure

China's government and media coined the term sheng nu ("leftover women") to describe educated, ambitious women over 27 who remain unmarried. The term, widely criticized as sexist, reflects intense social pressure on women to marry young. State media ran campaigns encouraging women not to be "too picky" and to prioritize marriage over career advancement.

The irony is striking: the one-child policy produced a generation of women who received unprecedented investment in their education (since families had only one child to invest in), and now these same women are stigmatized for being "too successful" to find husbands. Urban women are increasingly pushing back, rejecting traditional gender roles and the expectation that they should sacrifice career aspirations for domestic responsibilities.

Bride price: the economics of marriage

Bride price (caili) remains a significant tradition, particularly in rural China. The groom's family is expected to provide substantial financial contributions before marriage — typically including cash, gold jewelry, contributions toward housing, and sometimes a car. In some provinces, the expected bride price exceeds 200,000 yuan ($30,000), while the average rural household earns only 5,000 yuan per year from farming.

For many Chinese men, especially in rural areas, the bride price has become a barrier to marriage altogether. Some opt out of dating entirely to avoid the financial burden they know awaits them. The government has attempted to curtail excessive bride prices, viewing them as a contributor to declining marriage rates, but the practice remains deeply embedded in rural culture.

The gender imbalance

The one-child policy (1979-2015), combined with cultural preference for sons, created the world's most severe gender imbalance. At its peak, China's sex ratio at birth reached 121 boys for every 100 girls, with some provinces exceeding 130. Projections suggest that up to 50 million men may remain permanently unmarried — a cohort sometimes called "bare branches" (guang gun).

This imbalance has reshaped dating dynamics. In theory, it gives women more choice. In practice, it has intensified competition among men, inflated bride prices, and created a geographic mismatch: surplus men are concentrated in rural areas, while single women are concentrated in cities. The imbalance is expected to persist until at least 2050.

996 work culture and its impact on dating

China's notorious 996 work schedule — 9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week — has severely compressed the time and energy available for socializing, dating, and building relationships. Although China's Supreme Court ruled 996 illegal in 2021, enforcement has been inconsistent, and many tech and finance workers still endure grueling schedules. The result: a generation of urban professionals who are too exhausted to date, even if they want to.

WeChat-first communication

In Chinese dating, WeChat is everything. Exchanging WeChat IDs has replaced exchanging phone numbers as the first step in a potential relationship. Frequent WeChat messaging, sharing Moments (the platform's social feed), invitations to group activities, and small digital gestures (sending hongbao, or red envelope gifts) are all signals of interest. The platform functions as the primary infrastructure of courtship, from first contact through relationship maintenance.

Dating apps like Tantan, Momo, and Xiaohongshu serve as initial discovery platforms, but roughly 90% of connections migrate to WeChat quickly. The apps function more as funnels into WeChat than as standalone dating environments.

Government efforts to encourage dating

Facing a historic marriage and fertility crisis, the Chinese government has taken unprecedented steps to encourage young people to date and marry. Measures include organizing blind dating events and mass weddings, offering child subsidies (3,600 yuan per year per child until age 3), and even launching state-sponsored dating apps. In Jiangxi province, the government created "Palm Guixi," an app that uses data on single residents to organize blind dates.

Despite these efforts, marriage registrations hit a record low of 6.1 million in 2024 — a 20.5% plunge from the previous year. First marriages have fallen nearly 56% since 2015. The average age of first marriage now exceeds 30 in major cities. Young Chinese increasingly cite economic pressure, shifting gender expectations, and desire for personal freedom as reasons to delay or forgo marriage entirely.

Taboos and cultural sensitivities

Chinese dating culture carries several important sensitivities. Discussing finances openly is expected and not considered rude — income, property ownership, and family wealth are legitimate factors in partner evaluation, unlike in Western cultures where such directness would be taboo. Conversely, casual dating and hookup culture carry more stigma than in the West, particularly for women. Dating is widely viewed as a step toward marriage, not an end in itself. Living together before marriage, while increasingly common in cities, is still controversial in more traditional families and regions.
