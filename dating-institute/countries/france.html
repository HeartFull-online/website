France: When Even Romance Capital Falls

For decades, France was the exception. While Germany, Italy, and Spain watched their birth rates collapse, France held steady — hovering near replacement level with a TFR above 2.0 as recently as 2010. Generous family policies, subsidized childcare, long parental leave, and a culture that genuinely valued large families kept France as Europe's demographic success story.

That story is over. In 2025, deaths outnumbered births in France for the first time since the end of World War II. The country that proved pro-natalist policy could work is now proving that even the best policies aren't enough.

The fall from grace

France's total fertility rate peaked at 2.03 children per woman in 2010. By 2024, it had dropped to 1.61 — a 19% decline in just fourteen years. In 2025, it fell further to 1.56, the lowest level since the aftermath of World War I.

The raw numbers tell a stark story. In 2010, France recorded 833,000 births. By 2024, that number had fallen to 663,000 — a drop of 170,000 babies per year. In 2025, only 645,000 babies were born, 24% fewer than the 2010 peak, in a country with nearly 68 million people.

The model that stopped working

France's pro-natalist policies were not subtle. They were a deliberate, multi-generational national project dating back to the interwar period. The Family Code of 1939 introduced direct financial transfers to families with multiple children, tax benefits for larger households, and maternity grants. After WWII, the system expanded dramatically.

By the 2000s, France was spending 3.8% of GDP on family benefits — cash payments, services, and tax breaks — the highest level in the OECD, which averaged 2.9%. The Caisse d'Allocations Familiales (CAF) distributed billions in family support. Childcare spending reached 1.1% of GDP, well above the OECD average of 0.7%.

Research estimated these policies boosted French fertility by 0.1 to 0.2 children per woman, sustained across roughly 80 years of policy. Without them, France would have 5 to 10 million fewer people today.

But the policies that once made France exceptional are now merely slowing a decline that is happening everywhere. The infrastructure of family support remains intact. What changed is how people meet, date, and form relationships in the first place.

Macron's "demographic rearmament"

In January 2024, President Emmanuel Macron used an extraordinary phrase during a press conference at the Elysee Palace. He called for France's "demographic rearmament" — military language for what he framed as a national emergency. He pledged better parental leave and a campaign to combat infertility, which he called "the taboo of the century."

The response was a 16-measure plan covering prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and medical innovation — led by Health Minister Stephanie Rist. One proposal would send fertility-awareness letters to all French citizens turning 29.

The plan sparked an outcry. Feminists and left-wing politicians accused Macron of seeking to control women's bodies. The controversy was fierce, but the underlying numbers were not in dispute. A year later, in January 2026, INSEE confirmed the worst: 2025 was the year deaths overtook births.

Happn: born in Paris

France is not just a consumer of dating apps — it produced one. Happn was founded in Paris in 2013 by Didier Rappaport, Fabien Cohen, and Antony Cohen. Rappaport, a serial entrepreneur and co-founder of video-sharing platform Dailymotion, designed the app around a distinctly Parisian idea: what if you could reconnect with the stranger you crossed paths with on the Metro, in a cafe, on the street?

The concept was location-based matching — the app would show you people you had physically been near. It grew quickly: 40,000 daily users by July 2014, 10 million total users by January 2016. In September 2025, Happn was acquired by Hello Group, a Chinese social media company.

The trajectory is telling. A French startup built on the romance of real-world encounters was ultimately absorbed into the global dating app conglomerate machine. The serendipity it tried to digitize became another feed to swipe through.

The dating app market

France's online dating market is projected to reach 203 million euros in revenue in 2025, with a user penetration rate of approximately 11.2%. France ranks among the top countries globally for Tinder usage, alongside Brazil and Germany.

The dominant apps are familiar: Tinder leads, followed by Bumble and Happn. The same business model that drives the global industry — free to browse, pay to connect — operates identically in France. Premium subscriptions, boosts, super likes, and roses all gate meaningful interaction behind a paywall.

With roughly 8 million projected online dating users by 2029 in a country of 68 million, the penetration isn't as extreme as in the UK (16%) or the US. But it is growing, and it is increasingly the default way younger French people meet potential partners.

Why France matters

France is the strongest counterargument to the idea that policy alone can solve the fertility crisis. No country tried harder. No country spent more, as a share of GDP, on family support. And for decades, it worked — France's TFR remained significantly above its European neighbors.

But the decline since 2010 has erased much of that advantage. France's TFR of 1.61 is now nearly identical to the United States (1.63) and only slightly above the UK (1.55). The French exception is no longer exceptional.

Researchers at INSEE and INED have identified several contributing factors: economic uncertainty, rising housing costs, eco-anxiety, geopolitical instability, and delayed entry into parenthood. The risk of infertility rises sharply with age — affecting one in five couples at 30, one in three at 35, and one in two at 40.

What these analyses consistently undercount is how people form relationships in the first place. You can offer the most generous parental leave in Europe, but it doesn't matter if people never get to the point of becoming parents. The bottleneck has moved upstream — from supporting families to forming them.

The numbers in context

The 2025 milestone — more deaths than births — hadn't happened in France since 1945. The last time births were this low, France had nearly 30 million fewer people. The country is not just declining relative to its peak; it is declining in absolute terms that have no peacetime precedent.

France proves that the fertility crisis is not simply a policy failure or a cultural attitude. It is something structural about how modern societies organize intimacy and partnership. Dating apps did not cause this alone. But they are the infrastructure through which an entire generation now attempts to find love — and that infrastructure is optimized for engagement and revenue, not for the formation of lasting relationships.
