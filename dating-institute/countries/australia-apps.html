Dating App Landscape: Australia

Australia is one of the few countries in the world where Hinge has overtaken Tinder in weekly downloads. Yet Tinder still generates nearly three times Hinge's revenue — a split that reveals how deeply entrenched Tinder's monetization machine remains, even as users migrate to competitors.

The Hinge-first market

Australia is one of only a handful of countries globally where Hinge leads Tinder in weekly downloads. In most markets — the US, UK, Germany, France — Tinder still dominates the download charts. Australia's shift to Hinge is significant because it suggests that a critical mass of users has actively chosen a different model.

Hinge's 14,500 weekly downloads versus Tinder's 12,800 represent a meaningful gap, not a statistical tie. With 320,000 monthly active users, Hinge has built a substantial user base — though Tinder's 416,000 MAU shows it retains a larger installed base from years of dominance.

The revenue split is more telling. Tinder pulls in $1.19M per week — nearly three times Hinge's $410K. This isn't because Tinder is better. It's because Tinder has had a decade to build its paywall infrastructure: Gold, Platinum, Select, Boosts, Super Likes. Hinge's monetization is newer and less aggressive, which is partly why users prefer it — and partly why Match Group is pushing harder to monetize it.

Revenue versus satisfaction

The Australian market perfectly illustrates the dating app paradox: the app users prefer most is not the one that makes the most money. Tinder generates nearly triple Hinge's revenue despite losing the download race. Bumble, at #3 in both downloads and revenue, completes a three-app oligopoly that accounts for over 95% of market revenue.

Tinder extracts $2.86 per active user per week — more than double Hinge's $1.28. That gap reflects Tinder's more aggressive paywall strategy: features that used to be free now sit behind subscription tiers. Users are paying more, but they aren't staying happier. They're leaving for Hinge.

Also popular

Beyond the big three, several apps maintain niche positions in Australia. Happn, the proximity-based French app, has a loyal following in dense inner-city areas of Sydney and Melbourne. POF (Plenty of Fish) retains an older demographic that discovered it before Tinder existed. eHarmony targets the relationship-seeking segment willing to pay premium prices for compatibility-focused matching.

None of these challengers individually threatens the top three. Combined, they represent less than 5% of market revenue. The Australian dating app market is, for all practical purposes, a three-player game between two companies: Match Group (Hinge + Tinder) and Bumble Inc.

What this tells us

Australia proves that users will migrate when given a better option — but that migration doesn't escape the duopoly. When an Australian user leaves Tinder for Hinge, they move from one Match Group product to another. The revenue still flows to the same parent company. The structural incentive — keep users paying, not partnered — remains unchanged.

The Hinge-first market is not a sign that the system is self-correcting. It's a sign that users are desperate for something different — and the industry is adept at offering the appearance of change without changing the business model underneath.
