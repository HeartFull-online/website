Dating App Safety: China

China operates the most state-controlled dating app environment of any major market. Real-name registration is mandatory, personal data must stay on Chinese servers, and platforms are monitored as part of national security policy. Yet despite — or perhaps because of — this top-down control, romance scams and fake profiles remain pervasive.

Real-name registration

All Chinese internet services, including dating apps, require real-name registration. Regulations drawn up by the Ministry of Civil Affairs, the Communist Youth League, and the National Health and Family Planning Commission require dating platforms to keep records of users' real identities. In theory, this should eliminate anonymous fake profiles.

In practice, enforcement is inconsistent. Investigative journalists have demonstrated that it is possible to register on major dating sites using fake IDs with incorrect numbers and random photos. Users can also change personal information — such as academic background, profession, and salary — after sign-up without further verification. The gap between policy and enforcement creates a false sense of security.

PIPL and data localization

China's Personal Information Protection Law (PIPL), effective November 2021, imposes strict data localization requirements. All personal data collected within China must be stored on servers located in Chinese territory. Cross-border transfers require a security assessment by the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC), completion of standard contractual clauses, or certification by a CAC-approved provider.

For dating apps, this means that all user data — including messages, photos, location history, and preferences — must remain within China's borders and is accessible to Chinese authorities. Non-compliance carries penalties of up to 50 million yuan (~$7 million) or 5% of annual revenue. Violations are also recorded in the social credit system, which can restrict a company's ability to operate, access credit, or conduct business in China.

Government monitoring and oversight

Dating platforms in China operate under the broader framework of internet censorship and surveillance. The government views online dating as both a social tool and a potential national security concern. Authorities have issued warnings to Gen-Z citizens about risks associated with online dating as part of campaigns led by Xi Jinping to strengthen national security awareness.

Western dating apps like Tinder and Bumble are not banned outright but face significant barriers, including data localization requirements and content moderation rules that effectively limit their operations. The market is instead dominated by domestic apps like Tantan, Momo, and Baihe, which are fully compliant with government requirements.

Romance scams

Despite heavy regulation, organized romance scam operations flourish. A common pattern involves criminal groups operating dating app scams where men are deceived into paying for interactions with female hosts posing as friends or romantic partners. "Sha zhu pan" (pig-butchering) scams — where fraudsters build online romances before luring victims into fake cryptocurrency investments — have targeted both domestic and overseas Chinese communities. In one Hunan province case, police investigated an app where paid hosts systematically defrauded male users.

In response, the government announced a major clampdown on dating platforms after a high-profile case where a marriage scam led to a victim's suicide. The crackdown targeted fake identities and fraudulent profiles, though critics note that the government's own surveillance infrastructure has not been effective at preventing these crimes.

Social credit implications

China's social credit system intersects with dating app regulation in unique ways. PIPL violations by dating platforms are recorded in the corporate social credit system, potentially blocking the company from loans, property purchases, and general operations. For individual users, certain online behaviors — including fraud convictions linked to dating platforms — can affect personal social credit scores, with consequences ranging from travel restrictions to reduced access to financial services.

Tips for dating app users in China

Verify identity claims independently — do not trust profiles at face value despite real-name requirements. Never invest money based on a dating app contact's recommendation, especially in cryptocurrency. Be aware that all messages are potentially accessible to authorities. Use domestic apps with established reputations (Tantan, Baihe) rather than unknown platforms. Report scams to local police or the 12321 internet complaint hotline.
