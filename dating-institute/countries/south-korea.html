South Korea: The World's Lowest Birth Rate

South Korea's total fertility rate has fallen to 0.75 — the lowest of any country on Earth. Each generation is now roughly one-third the size of the previous one. No government policy, no amount of spending, and no dating app has reversed the trend.

The steepest decline on record

In 2007, South Korea's TFR was 1.26 — already well below the 2.1 replacement rate. By 2024, it had fallen to 0.75, a 40% collapse. No other developed nation has experienced a decline this severe or this fast.

At a TFR of 0.75, each generation is roughly one-third the size of the one before it. A country of 51 million today faces a future where its working-age population will shrink dramatically within decades. South Korea's median age jumped from 33.6 years in 2005 to 44.5 in 2023.

The sampo generation

The Korean term sampo sedae (“sampo generation”) literally means “the generation that has given up three things”: dating, marriage, and children. It emerged in the early 2010s to describe young Koreans crushed under overlapping pressures that make traditional life milestones feel impossible.

The term has since expanded. Opo sedae (five things) adds employment and home ownership. Chilpo sedae (seven things) adds social life and hope. N-po sedae drops the number entirely — young Koreans have given up on an indefinite number of life goals.

This is not apathy. It is a rational response to structural conditions:

  • Extreme academic pressure: 77% of students attend private universities with high tuition costs; the suneung college entrance exam dominates adolescence
  • Housing costs: The average Seoul apartment exceeds $720,000; total marriage costs average $220,000, with 70-80% going to housing
  • Overwork culture: South Korea has among the longest working hours in the OECD, leaving little time for relationships
  • Gender tensions: Women increasingly refuse to sacrifice careers for motherhood in a society where domestic labor still falls disproportionately on wives
  • Childcare costs: The average cost of raising a child is estimated at $275,000; national cram school spending exceeds $19 billion per year

Marriage in freefall

Since marriage remains a near-prerequisite for childbearing in South Korea (births outside marriage account for roughly 2-3% of all births, compared to 40% in France and the US), the marriage decline directly drives the fertility collapse.

The crude marriage rate has halved, falling from 8.7 per 1,000 people in 1995 to 4.4 in 2024. The average age at first marriage has risen sharply: men now marry at 33.9 (up from 28.4 in 1995) and women at 31.6 (up from 25.3). In Seoul, those figures climb to 34.4 and 32.4 respectively.

A growing share of young Koreans no longer view marriage as necessary at all. In surveys, the percentage of women aged 30-34 who have never married has risen steadily, and attitudes have shifted from “marriage is a must” to “marriage is optional.”

The dating app landscape

South Korea's dating app market is worth approximately $196 million, growing at 9.1% annually. Online dating penetration sits at 10.74% — lower than the US (17.6%) or UK (16.2%), but rising fast.

Unlike Western markets dominated by Tinder and Bumble, South Korea has a thriving local app ecosystem. The most popular platforms include:

A notable feature of Korea's dating app culture is its gatekeeping mechanisms. Amanda requires an appearance rating to join. Exclusive apps cater to graduates of top-tier universities (SKY schools) or employees of major conglomerates (chaebols). This mirrors the hyper-competitive culture — even finding a date is filtered through credentials and rankings.

Gender imbalance on these platforms is significant. Data shows Korean dating apps are overflowing with male users, though women tend to be more active users once on the platform.

$300 billion spent, little to show

Since 2006, the South Korean government has spent nearly $300 billion on pro-natalist policies: cash bonuses for newborns, subsidized childcare, housing benefits for young families, fertility treatment coverage, and matchmaking programs.

The fertility rate continued to fall through every year of this spending. The government has tried increasingly creative approaches:

  • Cash payments of up to $70,000+ per newborn
  • Subsidized government-run dating events
  • Housing priority for married couples
  • Extended parental leave policies
  • In 2024, creating a dedicated Ministry of Population to coordinate the response

There has been a modest recent uptick: births rose for 15 consecutive months through September 2025, with 190,000 babies born in the first nine months — a 6.9% year-over-year increase, the largest since 2007. But researchers caution this is driven by the larger cohort of women born in the early 1990s aging into their final childbearing years. Once they pass through, the smaller cohorts born after 1996 will take their place, and the rebound is expected to fade.

Research estimates that to bring fertility back to desired levels, pro-natalist benefit levels would need to be roughly 15 times larger than current programs. The gap between what the government offers and what it actually costs to raise a child in South Korea remains enormous.

What South Korea reveals

South Korea is the most extreme example of a pattern visible across every developed nation. It demonstrates that fertility collapse is not primarily about access to dating — apps are abundant and growing — but about the structural conditions surrounding relationships: housing, work, education costs, and gender equity.

It also demonstrates the limits of government intervention. Nearly $300 billion in spending has not reversed the trend. The pressures that keep young Koreans from forming families run deeper than any subsidy can reach.

Dating apps in this context serve a paradoxical role. They make it easier to meet people in a society where long work hours leave little time for organic socializing. But their monetization model — charging for connections, filtering by credentials, gamifying attraction — may reinforce the same competitive, transactional dynamics that already permeate Korean society. When even romance is scored, ranked, and paywalled, the sampo generation's decision to opt out starts to look less like giving up and more like a refusal to play a game that was never designed for them to win.
