Dating App Landscape: China

China's dating app ecosystem is unlike any other. The market is worth $1,497 million — second only to the US — but operates in a walled garden cut off from Western platforms. Tinder, Bumble, and Hinge are effectively absent. Instead, a domestic ecosystem of social-dating hybrids, marriage platforms, and government-run services has emerged under the Hello Group duopoly.

The Hello Group duopoly

Hello Group (NYSE: MOMO), formerly Momo Inc., controls the two dominant dating-adjacent platforms in China. Momo, launched in 2011, started as a location-based social app and evolved into a dating-entertainment hybrid where livestreaming generates the majority of revenue. Tantan, launched in 2014 as a Tinder-style swipe app, was acquired by Hello Group in 2018 for approximately $735 million.

This mirrors the Match Group model in the West — one company owning both the dominant social-dating platform and the dominant swipe-dating platform. The key difference is revenue composition. While Match Group earns almost entirely from subscriptions, Hello Group derives over 60% of revenue from livestreaming and virtual gifts. Users pay to watch performers, send virtual roses, and gain visibility — entertainment that keeps them on the app rather than off it.

Marriage platforms: a Chinese-only category

The West has no real equivalent to Baihe and Zhenai. These are marriage-first platforms where parents often create and manage profiles for their children. Baihe (merged with Jiayuan in 2017) has 85 million registered users and operates like a digitized version of traditional matchmaking. Zhenai goes further, maintaining physical matchmaking centers in major cities where paid matchmakers conduct interviews and arrange introductions.

These platforms charge significantly more than casual dating apps — Zhenai's premium matchmaking packages can cost hundreds or even thousands of dollars. The business model is aligned with outcomes in a way Western apps are not: matchmakers are incentivized to produce marriages because their reputation and referral business depend on it. Yet even these platforms struggle against the structural headwinds of housing costs, work culture, and shifting attitudes toward marriage.

Soul: the Gen-Z alternative

Soul, backed by Tencent, represents a different approach entirely. Users create avatar-based profiles rather than uploading photos. Matching is based on personality tests and interest tags rather than appearance. The app emphasizes "soul-level connections" and virtual socializing over traditional dating.

Soul has resonated with younger Chinese users who are exhausted by the appearance-focused culture of Tantan and the marriage pressure of Baihe. It has over 30 million monthly active users and filed for a US IPO (later withdrawn). The avatar model sidesteps some of the lookism problems that plague swipe apps, but it also creates its own issues — users sometimes feel deceived when they eventually meet in person.

Government intervention: unique to China

No other country has the government directly operating dating platforms. In China, multiple provincial chapters of the Communist Youth League have launched matchmaking apps and organized mass blind-date events. Some local governments offer cash subsidies for couples who marry before age 30 or who have multiple children.

The results have been underwhelming. Young Chinese are generally skeptical of state-run dating services, and the apps suffer from low engagement. The fundamental problem is not a lack of matchmaking tools — it is that the economic conditions for family formation have deteriorated faster than any app or subsidy can compensate. Average home prices in Beijing and Shanghai exceed 40 times the average annual salary. No dating app, state-run or otherwise, can fix that.

What this tells us

China's dating market demonstrates that the problem with dating apps is not cultural but structural. Despite a completely different cultural context, different apps, different ownership, and even direct government intervention, the same pattern holds: apps optimized for engagement over outcomes fail to produce relationships. Hello Group's livestreaming revenue model is arguably even more misaligned than Match Group's subscription model — at least subscriptions are nominally tied to finding a partner. Virtual gifts are pure entertainment.

The Chinese market also proves that you cannot solve a business model problem with policy. The government has tried subsidies, exhortation, and building its own platforms. None of it has moved the fertility rate. What China needs — and what every country in this research needs — is a dating model where the platform's financial incentives align with the user's relationship goals. That model does not yet exist at scale anywhere in the world.
