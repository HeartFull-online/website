Dating App Safety: Japan

Japan was one of the first countries to regulate online dating, passing its deai-kei law in 2003. Violent crime rates are among the lowest globally, but catfishing, marriage fraud, and romance scams are rising sharply. Complaints to the Tokyo Consumer Affairs Center jumped from 80 in 2019 to 809 in 2024 — a tenfold increase.

The deai-kei regulations

In 2003, Japan's National Diet passed the Law to Regulate Solicitation of Children through Matching Business via Internet Sites, targeting "deai-kei" (encounter/meeting sites). The law prohibits soliciting minors for prostitution and bans sexual solicitation messages on internet forums. Penalties include fines up to 100,000 yen and up to one year of imprisonment.

The law was strengthened in 2008 after a surge in sex crimes involving minors. The amendment increased penalties and required all website administrators to register with the Public Safety Commission. The National Police Agency reported 1,421 prosecutions in 2011 for crimes including child pornography and welfare violations linked to dating sites.

Age verification: mandatory by law

Unlike many countries, Japan legally requires dating apps to verify users' ages. Apps must confirm that users are 18 or older, typically through government-issued ID uploads. This requirement predates similar moves in the UK and US by over a decade, reflecting Japan's early awareness of how dating platforms can be exploited.

Marriage fraud and catfishing

A 2024 survey by Omicale Inc. of 1,064 dating app users found that 77.2% had encountered trouble. The top complaint was visual misrepresentation — outdated or heavily edited photos. More seriously, 6.8% of women and 3.5% of men discovered their match was hiding a spouse or existing partner.

Japan's courts are beginning to take action. In December 2025, a Tokyo District Court ordered a married man to pay 1.5 million yen (~$9,800) in damages to a woman he met on a dating app while concealing his marriage. This ruling signals growing legal accountability for dating app deception.

Government-backed dating apps

In a unique approach, the Tokyo Metropolitan Government launched Tokyo Futari Story, an official dating app requiring an extensive background check including identity verification and a copy of the user's family registry (koseki) to prove they are single. The app Tapple also began using My Number national ID cards to verify marital status — the first private company to do so.

Some rural prefectures have gone further, subsidizing dating app usage for single residents as a measure to combat Japan's declining birth rate. This government involvement represents a fundamentally different posture toward dating apps compared to the hands-off US approach.

Cultural barriers to reporting

Despite these protections, cultural stigma remains a barrier. Many victims — especially men — are reluctant to report dating-related fraud or harassment due to shame. Romance scam reports in Japan are believed to significantly undercount actual incidents. Foreign-origin scams targeting Japanese users have also surged, with organized groups using social media to lure victims into cryptocurrency fraud schemes.

Tips for dating app users in Japan

Look for verified badges. Use apps that require government ID verification. Request a koseki or marital-status check if the app supports it. Be wary of requests for money — especially cryptocurrency. Meet in public places and inform a friend. Report fraud to the nearest police station or the National Consumer Affairs Center (188 hotline).
