Red Flags: What to Watch For

Dating apps connect you to strangers at scale. Most people are genuine, but manipulative behavior is common enough that recognizing it early can save you months of damage. Here are the patterns to watch for.

1. Love bombing

What it looks like: Overwhelming affection, constant texting, grand declarations of connection within days. "I've never felt this way about anyone." "You're not like anyone I've ever met." Moving at a pace that feels dizzying but flattering. Planning trips together before you've had a third date. Talking about a future together before you know their middle name.

Why people miss it: It feels incredible. After months of mediocre matches and dead-end conversations, someone showing intense interest feels like finally finding "the one." The dopamine rush of constant attention overrides the rational voice saying "this is too fast." Research on intermittent reinforcement (Skinner, 1938) shows that the love-bombing phase creates a biochemical attachment that makes the inevitable withdrawal feel devastating.

What to do: Genuine connection builds gradually. If someone is telling you they love you after a week, they're not connecting with you as a person — they're projecting a fantasy. Slow down. A person with healthy intentions will respect a request to take things at a more measured pace. A love bomber will react with hurt, anger, or escalated intensity.

2. Breadcrumbing

What it looks like: Sporadic, low-effort contact designed to keep you interested without any actual commitment. Occasional texts ("thinking of you"), social media reactions, or vague plans that never materialize. They go quiet for days, then resurface with just enough attention to pull you back in. They respond to messages but never initiate. They hint at meeting up but never commit to a time and place.

Why people miss it: Each individual message seems fine. It's the pattern that reveals the problem. When you're hopeful about someone, you focus on the moments of contact rather than the long silences between them. Confirmation bias (Nickerson, 1998) leads you to interpret their rare messages as evidence of interest rather than a pattern of avoidance.

What to do: Judge behavior patterns, not individual messages. If someone consistently fails to make concrete plans or goes silent for days between replies, they're keeping you as an option, not treating you as a priority. Name the pattern: "I notice we keep talking about getting together but it doesn't happen. Are you actually interested in meeting?" Their response will tell you everything.

3. Future faking

What it looks like: Making elaborate plans and promises about a shared future that never materialize. "We should go to Italy this summer." "I can't wait for you to meet my family." "I'm going to cook you the best meal of your life next weekend." The plans feel exciting and specific, but they never happen — and when the time comes, there's always an excuse. A new, bigger plan replaces the one that fell through.

Why people miss it: Future faking creates emotional investment. When someone paints a vivid picture of a shared future, your brain starts to experience it as partially real (research on mental simulation by Taylor et al., 1998). You become invested in the relationship's potential rather than its reality. The gap between what's promised and what's delivered widens gradually, making it harder to see.

What to do: Track follow-through. Genuine interest shows up in actions, not words. If someone makes plans and follows through, great. If they consistently paint beautiful futures that never arrive, that's the data that matters. Pay attention to what actually happens, not what's promised.

4. Gaslighting

What it looks like: Making you question your own perception of reality. "I never said that." "You're being too sensitive." "That's not what happened." "You're imagining things." Denying things you clearly remember, reframing your reasonable reactions as overreactions, and gradually eroding your trust in your own judgment.

Why people miss it: Gaslighting works because it's gradual and because most people give others the benefit of the doubt. When someone confidently tells you your memory is wrong, you wonder if maybe it is. Research on social conformity (Asch, 1956) shows that people will doubt their own perceptions when contradicted by someone with confidence. On dating apps specifically, the early stages of a relationship involve so much uncertainty that it's easy to second-guess yourself.

What to do: Trust your memory. If you find yourself constantly questioning whether you said something or whether your feelings are valid, that is itself a red flag. Keep screenshots of conversations if something feels off. Talk to friends — an outside perspective can identify gaslighting that's invisible from inside.

5. Controlling behavior

What it looks like: Wanting to know where you are, who you're with, and why you didn't respond immediately. Getting upset when you spend time with friends. Commenting on what you wear, who you follow on social media, or how you spend your time. Framing control as concern: "I just worry about you." "I only get jealous because I care so much."

Why people miss it: In the early stages, controlling behavior often masquerades as intense interest. When someone wants to talk to you all the time, it feels like devotion. When they get jealous, it feels like passion. Research on coercive control (Stark, 2007) shows that controlling partners escalate gradually — they test boundaries with small requests before moving to larger ones. By the time the control is obvious, the emotional investment makes it hard to leave.

What to do: Healthy relationships involve trust. If someone is monitoring your behavior or getting angry about your autonomy — even early on — that pattern will intensify, not improve. Set a boundary and observe the reaction. A respectful person will adjust. A controlling person will push harder, get angry, or use guilt.

6. Financial requests

What it looks like: Any request for money, no matter how small or how reasonable the story. A sudden emergency, a car breakdown, a missed rent payment, an investment opportunity, requests for gift cards, or asking you to receive or send money on their behalf. Romance scams cost victims an estimated $1.3 billion in 2023 (FTC), making them the most financially damaging fraud category.

Why people miss it: By the time the financial request comes, the scammer has invested weeks or months building emotional connection. The request comes wrapped in vulnerability and trust. People who would never send money to a stranger will send it to someone they believe they're in a relationship with. The sunk cost fallacy (Arkes & Blumer, 1985) makes people continue investing in something they've already put time into, even when red flags appear.

What to do: Never send money to someone you haven't met in person, regardless of the circumstance. This rule has no exceptions. Legitimate romantic interests will never ask you for money early in a relationship. If they do, end contact immediately and report the profile to the app.

7. Rushing physical or emotional intimacy

What it looks like: Pushing for physical intimacy before you're comfortable. Insisting on meeting at their place instead of a public setting. Sending unsolicited explicit messages or photos. On the emotional side: demanding deep personal disclosures early, getting upset when you maintain boundaries, or framing your caution as a character flaw ("you're too guarded").

Why people miss it: Cultural scripts around dating normalize urgency. "When you know, you know" is treated as romantic wisdom rather than a justification for skipping the trust-building that healthy relationships require. Pressure is also easier to dismiss when it comes from someone you're attracted to.

What to do: Your comfort is not negotiable. Someone who respects you will respect your pace. If someone pressures you, that tells you how they'll handle future boundaries. A clear "I'm not comfortable with that yet" is a complete response. How they react to that response tells you everything you need to know about them.

8. Inconsistent stories

What it looks like: Details that don't add up. Their job title changes. The city they grew up in shifts. They mentioned siblings once but later say they're an only child. Their weekend plans don't match what they said they were doing. Their social media presence doesn't match what they've told you.

Why people miss it: Small inconsistencies are easy to explain away. "Maybe I misheard." "Maybe they were joking." People are generous with interpretations when they like someone. But a pattern of inconsistencies usually means either dishonesty or multiple simultaneous conversations where they can't keep stories straight.

What to do: Note inconsistencies without confrontation initially. If a pattern emerges, ask about it directly and non-accusatively: "I thought you mentioned X before, but now you're saying Y — did I misunderstand?" Their reaction matters more than their explanation. Defensiveness, deflection, or turning it around on you are all warning signs.

9. Refusing video calls

What it looks like: Consistently avoiding video calls or in-person meetings despite weeks of texting. Every suggestion to FaceTime or meet is met with an excuse: bad camera, too busy, shy, not ready yet. They're available for endless texting but never for anything that would confirm their identity.

Why people miss it: Some people genuinely are shy or camera-averse. But after weeks of conversation, a continued refusal to do a simple video call is a significant red flag. The FTC reports that inability to meet in person or video chat is the single most common characteristic of romance scam victims' experiences.

What to do: A brief video call before meeting in person is a reasonable request. Frame it as a safety measure, not a demand: "I like to do a quick video chat before meeting up — it's a safety thing for me." If they refuse repeatedly, they're either not who they say they are or not interested enough to accommodate a basic request. Either way, you have your answer.

10. Isolation tactics

What it looks like: Subtly or overtly discouraging your other relationships. Criticizing your friends ("they're a bad influence"), creating conflicts that coincide with your social plans, sulking when you spend time with others, or manufacturing situations where you have to choose between them and everyone else. Making you feel guilty for having a life outside the relationship.

Why people miss it: Isolation happens slowly. It often starts with seemingly reasonable requests — spending more time together, skipping one event to stay in, seeing less of a friend they've expressed concerns about. Each individual concession seems small. The National Domestic Violence Hotline identifies isolation as one of the earliest and most reliable predictors of abusive relationships.

What to do: Maintain your friendships and outside relationships, full stop. A healthy partner will encourage your independence, not threaten it. If someone consistently creates tension around your other relationships, that is a pattern of control. Talk to the people who know you best — isolation works precisely because it removes the outside perspectives that would help you see what's happening.

The common thread

Every red flag on this list shares one characteristic: a mismatch between words and behavior. Love bombers say "I love you" but don't know you. Breadcrumbers say "let's hang out" but never follow through. Future fakers promise the world but deliver nothing. Gaslighters say "I respect you" while undermining your reality.

The most reliable rule in dating is simple: pay attention to what people do, not what they say. Behavior is data. Words are claims. When they conflict, believe the behavior.

If you're experiencing any of these patterns and feel unsafe, contact the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233) or text START to 88788.
