Beyond "Hey": Conversations That Lead Somewhere

The first message is the highest-stakes moment in online dating. Here's what the data says about what works, what doesn't, and how to move from the app to an actual date.

1. Why "hey" doesn't work (the data)

"Hey" is the most common opening message on every dating app. It's also one of the least effective. Data from multiple platforms paints a clear picture:

Opening Message TypeResponse RateSource
"Hey" / "Hi" / "Hello"25%Hinge internal data
Generic compliment ("You're beautiful")30%OkCupid blog
Profile-specific comment50%Hinge internal data
Profile-specific question62%Hinge internal data
Humorous/creative opener45%Bumble data

The problem with "hey" isn't that it's rude — it's that it puts all the effort on the other person. They have to decide whether to engage, then figure out what to talk about, then carry the conversation. Most people, especially those with many matches, won't do that work for someone who didn't bother to read their profile.

The underlying principle: Messages that demonstrate you read someone's profile and found something specifically interesting get roughly 2.5x the response rate of generic openers. This isn't complicated, but most people don't do it.

2. What actually gets replies

Reference their profile

The single most effective strategy is mentioning something specific from their profile. Not "nice profile" — something that shows you actually read it and found it interesting. This works because of the psychological principle of personal recognition (Cialdini, 2006): people respond positively to evidence that they've been seen as an individual, not treated as interchangeable.

Low effort
"Hey, cute dog!"
High effort
"Is that a Bernese mountain dog? I grew up with one — do yours also think they're lap dogs despite weighing 100 pounds?"

The difference: the first message acknowledges the photo but gives nothing to respond to. The second references the photo, shares something personal, and asks a specific question that's easy and fun to answer.

Ask specific questions

Open-ended but specific questions dramatically outperform yes/no questions. The question should be easy to answer and interesting to think about. Research on conversational engagement (Huang et al., 2017) shows that questions which invite storytelling create the strongest sense of connection.

Too broad
"So what do you do for fun?"
Specific and engaging
"You mentioned you love hiking — what's the best trail you've done recently? I'm always looking for new ones."

Use humor (carefully)

Humor is powerful but risky in text. Without tone of voice and facial expressions, jokes can land wrong. Data from Bumble suggests humorous openers get a 45% response rate — higher than generic messages but lower than personalized questions. The key is that the humor should be light and inclusive, not edgy or self-deprecating to an extreme.

Works
"Your prompt says you're competitive about board games. I need to know — are we talking 'friendly rivalry' competitive or 'flip the Monopoly board' competitive? Because I need to prepare mentally."

The humor works because it references their profile, shows personality, and creates a natural opening for them to respond with their own story.

Share something about yourself

The best opening messages aren't pure questions — they include a small piece of self-disclosure. Research on reciprocal self-disclosure (Aron et al., 1997) shows that sharing something about yourself makes the other person more likely to share in return. A message that asks a question AND reveals something about the sender creates a conversational dynamic rather than an interrogation.

Question + self-disclosure
"I just finished the book you mentioned in your prompt — I couldn't put it down. Have you read anything else by that author? I'm on a kick."

3. The ideal message length

OkCupid published data on message length and response rates that has been corroborated by data from other platforms. The sweet spot is clear:

Message LengthResponse Rate
1-2 words ("Hey")25%
1 sentence (10-20 words)38%
2-3 sentences (20-60 words)52%
1 paragraph (60-100 words)44%
Multiple paragraphs (100+ words)32%

The sweet spot is 2-3 sentences — roughly 20-60 words. Long enough to show effort and personality, short enough to feel casual and low-pressure. Messages that are too long feel intense and demand an equally long response, which creates friction.

The ideal first message formula:

Sentence 1: Reference something specific from their profile. Sentence 2: Share a brief related thought or experience of your own. Sentence 3: Ask a specific, easy-to-answer question. Total: 25-50 words.

4. Timing: when to message and how fast to reply

Hinge data shows that sending a message within 24 hours of matching significantly increases the chance of a response. Matches that go un-messaged for more than 3 days rarely convert to conversations. The excitement of a new match fades quickly.

As for reply timing during a conversation, the data is less prescriptive. The "wait X hours before replying" advice is largely debunked — research on texting behavior (Scissors et al., 2016) suggests that consistent response times matter more than deliberately slow ones. If you're genuinely busy, reply when you can. If you're deliberately waiting to seem less interested, that strategy backfires: data from Bumble shows that conversations where both people reply within a few hours are 3x more likely to lead to a date than those with day-long gaps.

Best times to send first messages: Evenings (7-10pm) and Sunday afternoons show the highest response rates across platforms. Monday mornings and Friday nights show the lowest. This makes intuitive sense — people browse dating apps when they're relaxed and open, not when they're stressed or out with friends.

5. When to suggest meeting up

This is where most dating app conversations die. Two people have a pleasant exchange for days or weeks, but neither suggests meeting, and the conversation slowly fizzles. Hinge's data shows the average time from match to first date is 14 days, but their internal research suggests the optimal window is shorter.

The research-backed timeline:

  • Messages 1-5: Establish common ground and basic rapport.
  • Messages 5-10: Deepen the conversation slightly. You should have a sense of whether you'd enjoy talking to this person in real life.
  • Messages 10-15: Suggest meeting. This is the sweet spot. Enough conversation to feel comfortable, not so much that you've exhausted topics or built an unrealistic mental image.

Research on expectation vs. reality in online dating (Ramirez & Wang, 2008) found that people who met sooner (within 17-23 days of first contact) reported better date experiences than those who waited longer. Extended pre-meeting communication creates idealized expectations that real people can't match.

How to suggest it

Direct and specific beats vague. "We should hang out sometime" is easy to agree with and equally easy to never follow up on. A concrete suggestion with a time and activity is harder to dodge and easier to say yes to.

Vague
"We should get together sometime!"
Specific
"You mentioned that coffee shop on 5th — want to check it out Saturday afternoon? I've been meaning to go."

The specific version ties into something from the conversation, suggests a concrete time, and makes it easy to say yes. If Saturday doesn't work, they'll likely counter with another day. If they don't counter, you have your answer.

6. Moving from the app to real life

The transition from app to phone number or social media is a common source of anxiety. Some guidelines based on data and common practice:

  • Don't rush the number exchange. Asking for a phone number in the first few messages feels pushy and is a common scammer tactic. Wait until you've established rapport and a date is planned.
  • A video call bridges the gap. If either person is nervous about meeting a stranger, a 10-minute video call eliminates the biggest uncertainty: "Are they who they say they are?" Research shows video calls before first dates reduce no-shows and improve date satisfaction.
  • Meet in public first. This is a safety fundamental. Coffee shops, bars, and restaurants are ideal. Suggesting their apartment or yours as a first meeting spot is a red flag — see our red flags guide.
  • Keep it low-stakes. "Let's grab coffee" is better than "Let me take you to dinner." Lower investment means lower pressure and an easier yes.

7. When it's not working

Not every match will lead to a conversation, and not every conversation will lead to a date. That's normal. Some signs a conversation isn't going anywhere:

  • One-word responses. If they're answering "haha" and "yeah" and "nice" without asking anything back, they're not invested.
  • Consistently slow replies. If they take 2-3 days to respond to every message, the interest level is low.
  • No questions back. Conversation is reciprocal. If you're doing all the asking and they're doing all the answering, there's an effort imbalance that won't improve on a date.
  • Deflecting meeting suggestions. If you've suggested meeting twice and gotten vague non-commitments both times, move on.

Research on rejection sensitivity (Downey & Feldman, 1996) shows that people often interpret ambiguous signals as interest when they're hoping for it. If you have to ask "are they interested?" the answer is usually no. Interested people make it easy.

The best opening message in the world won't matter if you're not a good match. The point of messaging isn't to convince someone to like you — it's to find out whether there's genuine mutual interest. A conversation that dies is just information: this wasn't the right fit. The right fit won't need convincing.
