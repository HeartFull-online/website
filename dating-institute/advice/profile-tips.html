How to Build a Dating Profile That Works

Your dating profile is a first impression at scale. Here's what the data actually says about which photos, bios, and prompts get results — and what doesn't work despite being endlessly recommended.

1. Photo selection: what the data shows

Photos are the single most important element of a dating profile. Hinge's internal data (published in their "Data, A Love Story" reports) and independent research from Photofeeler provide clear patterns on what works and what doesn't.

The group photo debate

Group photos are controversial. Hinge data shows they decrease likes by about 20% when used as a primary photo, but that doesn't mean you should avoid them entirely. A group photo as your 3rd or 4th image shows you have friends and a social life — which matters. The rule: you should be immediately identifiable in every photo. If someone can't tell which person you are within one second, the photo is hurting you.

How many photos should you use?

Data from multiple apps converges on 5-6 photos as the optimal number. Profiles with fewer than 3 photos get significantly fewer matches — they signal low effort or something to hide. Profiles with the maximum (often 6-9) don't perform worse, but the marginal benefit of photo 7, 8, and 9 is near zero. Quality matters far more than quantity.

The ideal photo lineup:

1. Clear face shot, natural light, smiling. 2. Full body, doing something you enjoy. 3. Social/activity photo. 4. Something that shows your personality (travel, hobby, cooking). 5. Another clear face shot in a different setting. 6. Optional: with a pet, at an event, or a candid laugh.

2. Writing a bio that stands out

Most dating bios are interchangeable. "Love to travel, food enthusiast, looking for my partner in crime" describes roughly 40 million people. Research on distinctiveness in social perception (von Hippel & Trivers, 2011) shows that specific details are more memorable and more attractive than generic claims.

Specific beats generic

Compare these two bios:

"I love to travel, try new restaurants, and hang out with friends. Looking for someone who doesn't take themselves too seriously."

vs.

"Currently perfecting my sourdough recipe (month 6, still mediocre). Will argue passionately that the best meal I've ever had was at a gas station in New Mexico. Reading three books at once because I can't commit to one."

The second bio tells you something real about a person. It gives you something to respond to. It's specific enough that you couldn't copy-paste it onto anyone else's profile. That specificity is the entire point.

Humor works — but only if it's genuine

A study published in Evolution and Human Behavior (Greengross & Miller, 2011) confirmed that humor is one of the strongest predictors of attractiveness in both men and women. But the humor has to be your actual sense of humor, not a joke copied from the internet. Self-deprecating humor works well in moderation. Mean humor backfires. Observational humor about dating itself is overused.

What to avoid in your bio

  • Negativity. "Don't message me if..." or "Tired of games" immediately signals bitterness. Data from OkCupid's blog showed negative language reduces incoming messages by 30%.
  • Lists of demands. Height requirements, income expectations, or long lists of deal-breakers make you seem demanding before anyone has even spoken to you.
  • Nothing at all. An empty bio reduces match rates by 30-50% depending on the platform. It signals either laziness or that you're not taking this seriously.
  • Cliches. "Fluent in sarcasm," "love to laugh," "just ask" — these are so common they communicate nothing.
  • Excessive emoji. A couple are fine. A bio that's 50% emoji reads as low-effort on most platforms.
The 3-line bio formula:

Line 1: Something specific you're into right now (a project, obsession, or recent experience). Line 2: A quirk, opinion, or taste that reveals personality. Line 3: An easy hook for someone to start a conversation.

3. Prompt answers that start conversations

Apps like Hinge, Bumble, and Coffee Meets Bagel use prompt-based profiles. These prompts are conversation starters by design — but most people waste them with generic answers.

Hinge's data shows that the most effective prompt answers share three characteristics:

  • They're specific enough to reply to. "I'm looking for someone who..." followed by vague qualities doesn't give anyone an opening. "I'm looking for someone who'll try the weird item on any menu" gives them something to respond to.
  • They reveal something real. The best prompts share a genuine preference, experience, or opinion. "My most controversial opinion is that..." followed by an actual opinion beats a safe, crowd-pleasing answer.
  • They're the right length. One-word answers feel lazy. Paragraph-long answers feel intense. 1-3 sentences is the sweet spot that Hinge data supports.

Prompt selection matters too. Choose prompts that let you show personality, not ones that invite generic responses. "A life goal of mine" tends to produce boring answers. "I'll know it's time to delete this app when..." or "The way to win me over is..." tend to produce more engaging, distinctive responses.

4. Verification: why it matters

Photo verification (available on Tinder, Bumble, Hinge, and most major apps) has measurable benefits. Bumble reported that verified profiles receive up to 50% more matches than unverified ones. The reason is trust: verification signals that you are who your photos show, which reduces the catfishing anxiety that pervades online dating.

Beyond match rates, verification also changes who you match with. Verified profiles are more likely to match with other verified profiles, which correlates with higher response rates and more actual dates. It takes 30 seconds and costs nothing. There is no reason not to do it.

5. Putting it all together

The research on dating profiles points to a consistent theme: authenticity and specificity outperform optimization and performance. The profiles that get the most meaningful engagement aren't the most polished — they're the ones that feel like a real person wrote them.

A practical checklist:

  • 5-6 photos, variety of settings, natural light, clear face in at least 2
  • At least one full-body photo
  • No more than one group photo, and make sure you're identifiable
  • A bio with specific details, not generic claims
  • Prompt answers that give people something to respond to
  • Verification enabled
  • No sunglasses in your primary photo
  • Recent photos (within the last year)
The goal of a good dating profile isn't to appeal to everyone. It's to clearly communicate who you are so the right people are drawn in and the wrong people self-select out. A profile that gets fewer but better matches is more valuable than one that gets more matches but fewer real connections.
