How Men Can Write Messages Women Actually Want to Read

Women receive 10–50x more messages than men on dating apps. Your message competes with dozens. Here's how to write one that gets read, gets a reply, and starts an actual conversation.

Understand the environment you're messaging into

Before we talk about what to write, you need to understand what her inbox looks like. A woman with a reasonably complete profile on Hinge or Bumble typically receives 20–50 likes per day. On Tinder, it can be more. Each one is a message she could respond to.

Most of those messages look like this: "Hey." "Hi beautiful." "What's up." "You're gorgeous." A wall of identical, zero-effort greetings. She's not ignoring your "hey" because she's rude. She's ignoring it because she received 30 of them today and there's no way to distinguish yours from the rest.

Your goal isn't to write the cleverest message ever composed. It's to write one that clearly shows you read her profile and that makes it easy for her to respond. That alone puts you ahead of 90% of what she's seeing.

Why copy-paste doesn't work

Some men's dating advice recommends writing a "killer opener" and sending it to every match. The logic seems sound: it's efficient, you can A/B test, and a good line should work on anyone.

In practice, it fails. Women can tell. Not because they're psychic, but because generic messages feel generic. "If you were a vegetable, you'd be a cute-cumber" might get a response once, but it signals that you didn't read anything about her, didn't care enough to engage with who she is, and are running a volume play. That's not flattering — it's the opposite.

The men who get the best response rates are not sending the most messages. They're sending fewer, better, more specific messages. It takes 60 seconds to read someone's profile and write a message that references something in it. That 60 seconds dramatically changes your odds.

What "hey" actually communicates

"Hey" is the most common first message on every dating app. It is also the message with the lowest response rate. Here's what "hey" actually tells her:

  • You didn't read her profile
  • You're not willing to put in effort before you've even met
  • You expect her to do the work of starting a conversation
  • She's interchangeable to you — you sent this to 20 other women today

None of this is necessarily true about you. But it's what "hey" signals. And in a context where she's choosing between 30 messages, signals matter.

A message doesn't have to be brilliant. It has to show you saw her as a specific person, not a profile in a queue. That's a surprisingly low bar, and most men don't clear it.

How to reference her profile specifically

The simplest effective messaging strategy is: look at her profile, find something specific, ask about it. Not a generic compliment about something specific ("nice hiking photo!") but a genuine question or observation that shows you engaged.

Low-effort (generic)
"Hey, love your travel photos!"
This could be sent to anyone with travel photos. It requires no effort and gives her nothing to respond to.
Better (specific)
"That photo in Lisbon looks incredible. Did you take the tram up to Alfama or walk? I've been planning a trip and can't decide."
Shows you looked at her photos carefully, noticed a specific one, and asked a question she can easily answer. The personal detail (planning your own trip) gives her something to work with too.
Low-effort (generic compliment)
"You have a beautiful smile :)"
She's heard this hundreds of times. It's about her appearance, not her as a person.
Better (responding to content)
"OK wait, your prompt says pineapple belongs on pizza? I need to know: is this a firm position or are you open to negotiation?"
Engages with something she wrote. Playful without being try-hard. Easy to respond to. Shows you read her profile.

Message length: the sweet spot

OkCupid published data on optimal message length. The findings:

  • Too short (1–5 words): Gets ignored. "Hey" and "What's up" have the lowest response rates of any message type.
  • Sweet spot (40–90 words): Two to three sentences. Enough to show personality and ask a question, short enough that it doesn't feel like homework to read.
  • Too long (150+ words): Response rates drop. A paragraph-long message from a stranger feels intense. It suggests you're more invested than is appropriate for someone you haven't met. She doesn't owe a matching essay in response, and the pressure of that can prevent a response entirely.

The formula is simple: one sentence that references her profile specifically, one sentence that shares something related about you, and one question. That's it. Three sentences. 40–80 words. Done.

When humor works vs. when it's cringe

Humor is the most common advice for messaging. "Be funny!" But humor is context-dependent, and what's funny between friends is often uncomfortable from a stranger.

Humor that works

  • Self-deprecating (lightly). "I'd offer to cook for you but I should be honest that my signature dish is slightly burnt scrambled eggs." This is endearing because it's self-aware without being insecure.
  • Observational. Noticing something funny about her prompt answer or a shared experience. "Your 'green flag' prompt says 'owns a library card' — setting the bar exactly where it should be."
  • Absurdist, gently. "Your dog in that third photo has the energy of someone who just got promoted. I respect it."

Humor that doesn't

  • Sexual humor from a stranger. You haven't earned this level of familiarity. It reads as aggressive, not funny.
  • Negging or backhanded compliments. "You're pretty cute for someone who likes country music" is not charming. It's condescending.
  • Memes or copy-paste jokes. She's seen them. They signal "I send this to everyone."
  • Trying too hard. If a message takes 15 minutes to craft because you're workshopping the perfect witty line, it will probably read as forced. Casual and genuine beats clever every time.

Handling no response

You sent a thoughtful message. She didn't reply. What now?

Move on. That's the entire answer.

Not responding is a response. It means no. Maybe she was busy. Maybe she met someone else. Maybe she opened the app for five minutes, got overwhelmed by 40 notifications, and closed it. The reason doesn't matter. She doesn't owe you a reply, and sending a follow-up message ("guess you're too busy to respond") or a guilt trip ("I thought we had a connection based on your profile") is the fastest way to confirm she was right not to respond.

One follow-up is acceptable — but only if it's been several days and only if it's light and pressure-free. Something like "No worries if you're not interested, but I realized I never asked — did that pottery class you mentioned in your bio end up being as chaotic as it sounded?" Give it one more chance, and then genuinely let it go.

The hardest part of dating app messaging is accepting that most conversations won't happen. That's true for everyone, but especially for men. At a ~30% reply rate, you need to send roughly 3-4 thoughtful messages to get one response. That's the baseline. Don't take it personally — take it as math.

The quality of your messages matters more than the quantity. Five thoughtful, specific messages will outperform fifty copy-paste openers. Every time.

Once she responds: keeping the conversation alive

You got a reply. Now the instinct is often to either panic-text everything or play it artificially cool with delayed responses. Neither works.

  • Actually read what she wrote. Respond to what she said, not just the topic. If she shared a personal detail, acknowledge it. Don't just pivot to the next question on your mental list.
  • Share about yourself too. A conversation is a two-way exchange. If you only ask questions, it feels like an interview. If you only talk about yourself, it feels like a monologue. Match her energy: question, share, question, share.
  • Move to a date relatively quickly. App conversations that go on for more than a week without meeting tend to fizzle. After 5–10 exchanges where you're both clearly enjoying the conversation, suggest meeting. "This has been really fun — would you want to grab coffee sometime this week?"
  • Don't overthink timing. Reply when you see the message and have time to respond thoughtfully. The "wait three hours to seem less eager" strategy is exhausting for both people and doesn't actually signal anything positive.

The bottom line

Good messaging isn't a skill reserved for naturally charismatic people. It's a practice. Read her profile. Reference something specific. Ask a question. Keep it short. Be genuine. Accept that many conversations won't materialize. Focus your energy on the ones that do.

When you do connect with someone, the conversation will feel easy — because it was built on actual mutual interest, not a trick.
