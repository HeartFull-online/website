Setting Standards Without Settling (Or Being Unrealistic)

The hardest part of dating apps isn't finding matches. It's knowing which ones to pursue when the options feel endless. Here's what research says about making better decisions.

The paradox of choice

In 2000, psychologist Barry Schwartz documented something counterintuitive: more options make people less satisfied with their choices. His research showed that when people are presented with too many alternatives, they experience more anxiety, more regret, and less happiness with whatever they eventually choose.

Dating apps are the paradox of choice on steroids. With thousands of potential matches in any mid-sized city, the feeling that someone better might be one swipe away is constant. This isn't a personal failing — it's a predictable psychological response to the design of these platforms. Apps benefit from you always feeling like the next match might be The One, because that's what keeps you swiping.

The result for many women is a cycle: match with someone promising, start chatting, notice a small imperfection, wonder if someone better is out there, go back to swiping. Repeat. The apps get more engagement; you get decision paralysis.

Dealbreakers vs. preferences

One of the most useful things you can do before opening a dating app is get clear on the difference between your dealbreakers and your preferences. They're not the same thing, and treating preferences like dealbreakers is one of the fastest ways to stay single indefinitely.

Dealbreakers: the non-negotiables

Dealbreakers are values, behaviors, or life circumstances that would make a relationship fundamentally unworkable for you. They're usually about how someone treats people, what they want from life, and whether your trajectories are compatible.

  • Values alignment. Do they want kids? Are their political or religious views compatible with yours? Do they share your views on monogamy? These are structural questions that no amount of chemistry can resolve.
  • Character traits. Honesty, kindness, emotional maturity, accountability. Research consistently identifies these as the strongest predictors of relationship satisfaction. A landmark study by Gottman Institute found that contempt — disrespect, mockery, eye-rolling — is the single strongest predictor of divorce.
  • Safety and respect. This should go without saying, but: someone who doesn't respect your boundaries, belittles you, or makes you feel unsafe is a dealbreaker, full stop.

Preferences: the nice-to-haves

Preferences are things you'd like in a partner but that don't determine relationship viability. Height, specific hobbies, career prestige, music taste, how they dress. These are the things that dating apps train you to filter on because they're easy to turn into checkboxes.

The research is clear: the traits people say they want in a partner are poor predictors of who they actually like when they meet in person. A study published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology found that people's stated preferences (tall, ambitious, artistic) had almost no correlation with who they were actually attracted to on dates. Chemistry is not something you can filter for in advance.

Hold your dealbreakers firmly. Hold your preferences loosely. The person who makes you laugh, challenges your thinking, and shows up consistently might not check every box on your list — and that's exactly the point.

How apps encourage checklist thinking

Dating apps present people as a collection of attributes: height, job, education, a few photos, some text. This format naturally encourages a shopping mentality, where you evaluate each attribute against your ideal and reject anyone who falls short on any dimension.

In real life, attraction doesn't work like that. You meet someone through friends, at work, at an event — and you evaluate the whole person. Their humor, their energy, how they treat the waiter, whether they listen when you talk. These qualities are invisible on a profile. But they're the ones that actually determine whether you'll be happy with someone in six months, six years, or six decades.

This doesn't mean profiles don't matter or that you should swipe right on everyone. It means you should be aware that the format is inherently reductive, and compensate by giving more weight to conversation quality and in-person chemistry than to profile checkboxes.

The "spark" vs. "slow burn" debate

Dating culture puts enormous emphasis on the "spark" — instant chemistry, butterflies, that feeling of knowing right away. The research on this is mixed, and worth understanding.

What the spark actually is

The instant spark is primarily a combination of physical attraction and novelty. It feels like connection, but it's often just excitement. Research from the University of Toronto found that the intensity of initial attraction has almost no correlation with long-term relationship satisfaction. The people who reported the strongest sparks on first dates were no more likely to be in satisfying relationships a year later.

The case for slow burns

Many successful, deeply satisfying relationships started without fireworks. They started with "huh, that was pleasant" and grew from there. Studies show that attraction can and does develop over time, especially when it's based on personality compatibility rather than physical response.

This doesn't mean you should force yourself to date people you're not attracted to. It means that if a first date was pleasant, the conversation flowed, and you felt respected — a second date might be worth it even if your heart didn't skip a beat. Chemistry can develop. Character can't.

When to give someone a second date

  • Yes: The conversation was easy and you felt comfortable. They were respectful and curious. You left thinking "that was nice" even if not "wow."
  • Yes: You were nervous and didn't feel like your best self. First dates are high-pressure situations. Give both of you a second chance to be more relaxed.
  • No: You felt physically unsafe or disrespected. You don't owe a second chance to someone who made you uncomfortable.
  • No: They talked only about themselves, showed no curiosity about you, or displayed values that conflict with yours. These patterns don't improve.
  • Maybe: You're not sure. That uncertainty itself can be worth exploring. People who are "fine" on a first date sometimes become fascinating on a second, once the awkwardness fades.

What actually predicts relationship satisfaction

Decades of relationship research have identified what actually matters for long-term happiness. None of these things are visible in a dating profile, which is exactly the problem with filter-heavy dating.

  1. Emotional responsiveness. Does your partner respond when you reach out emotionally? Do they notice when you're upset? Do they care? Research by Sue Johnson identifies this as the foundation of secure attachment in adult relationships.
  2. Conflict style. Happy couples aren't couples who never fight — they're couples who fight well. Gottman's research shows that it's not whether you argue but how you argue that predicts satisfaction. Contempt, criticism, defensiveness, and stonewalling are the "four horsemen" that destroy relationships.
  3. Shared meaning-making. Do you share a sense of purpose? Do you have rituals and traditions you build together? These create the feeling of being a team, which research links to both relationship satisfaction and longevity.
  4. Humor compatibility. Not "do you both like memes" but "can you laugh together when things are hard?" Shared humor is one of the strongest predictors of long-term satisfaction, consistently outranking physical attractiveness.
  5. Growth orientation. Partners who see challenges as opportunities to grow together, rather than as signs that something is wrong, report higher satisfaction across every study that measures it.
Dating apps optimize for attributes you can see in a profile. Relationship satisfaction depends on qualities you can only discover through experience. The gap between these two things is the central challenge of modern dating.

Being open without compromising

"Lower your standards" is bad advice. "Examine your standards" is good advice. The difference is important.

You should never compromise on how someone treats you, whether they respect your autonomy, or whether your fundamental life goals are compatible. These are not negotiable and anyone who tells you otherwise is wrong.

But if your filter list has become so specific that it eliminates 99% of the population — if you're rejecting people for being 5'10" instead of 6'0", for working in marketing instead of tech, for liking hiking instead of climbing — it's worth asking whether those filters are serving you or just giving you a sense of control in a chaotic process.

The most helpful reframe: instead of "does this person check my boxes?" try "could I see myself growing with this person?" Growth is about trajectory, not a snapshot. Someone who is curious, kind, and headed in a direction compatible with yours might surprise you — even if they wouldn't have made it past your filters.
