The Revenue Mountain

As individual dating apps grew from startups to billion-dollar businesses, birth rates fell in lockstep. Here's the data, app by app.

Three apps, one trajectory

Tinder launched in 2012 with zero revenue. By 2024, it made $1.96 billion. Hinge went from $2 million to $691 million in eight years. Bumble from nothing to $866 million. Together, these three apps alone generate over $3.5 billion annually.

Over the same period, US fertility fell from 1.84 to 1.53 — a 17% decline. The lines are a mirror image.

Revenue: Tinder (DemandSage/Statista), Hinge (Match Group SEC filings), Bumble (Bumble Inc SEC filings). Fertility: World Bank.

What this data shows

Three observations stand out:

1. The revenue mountain is still growing, even as user satisfaction collapses. Despite 1.5-star Trustpilot averages, 78% user exhaustion, and 79% Gen Z fatigue — revenue kept climbing until 2023. Users hate the experience but have no alternative. That's a monopoly dynamic, not a quality signal.

2. Hinge's explosive growth didn't help fertility — it accelerated the same model. Hinge was supposed to be "designed to be deleted." Instead, it became Match Group's growth engine at $691M/year, using the same paywall-connections model. Better product design, same broken incentives.

3. Bumble's decline in 2024-2025 isn't a correction — it's the market saturating. Bumble revenue dropped from $1.07B (2023) to $866M (2024). Users are leaving, but they're not finding love — they're just giving up. The apps failed them, and now even the apps are failing.

The dating app industry grew from $47 million to $3.4 billion in a decade. Over the same decade, the US fertility rate dropped 17% to a historic low. Correlation isn't causation — but when the primary way people meet is optimized for revenue instead of connection, the outcomes speak for themselves.
