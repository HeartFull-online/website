The Real Problem Nobody Talks About

How dating apps commodify human connection, exploit their own users, and distort society at scale.

Your photos. Your stories. Their $4.5 billion.

Dating apps don't create any content. Every photo, every bio, every personality prompt, every conversation — that's all you. The platforms are empty shells without their users. They contribute nothing to the value that attracts people to the app.

Yet Match Group — the company behind Tinder, Hinge, OkCupid, Match.com, and Plenty of Fish — made $3.5 billion in revenue in 2025. Bumble Inc (Bumble + Badoo) added another $966 million. Combined, that's $4.5 billion a year extracted from user-generated content.

They use your attractiveness, your personality, your effort as bait to sell subscriptions to others. Beautiful profiles lure people in. The platform then charges those people $40–$80/month for the privilege of actually connecting. You are the product. You just aren't getting paid.

Money buys advantage. That's the design.

Every major dating app gates its most important features behind a paywall. Pay $50/mo and your profile gets boosted. Pay more and you see who liked you. Pay even more and your messages land at the top.

Can't afford it? You get buried. The algorithm literally suppresses your visibility to incentivize you to pay.

The person who can pay $80/mo to Bumble Premium+ has a measurably better chance of finding a partner than someone who can't afford it. Your dating success — arguably one of the most important outcomes in life — is determined by your income.

People become a commodity.

When you can buy access to people — buy the right to see them, message them, stand out to them — people become products with a price tag.

“See who liked you for $39.99.” That's a human being behind that paywall. Their interest in you, monetized by a platform that contributed nothing to the interaction. Their attention, sold. This is what commodification of human connection looks like.

It's one thing when a product costs money. It's another when people themselves are the product being sold.

If this is how society meets, it affects all of society.

If dating apps are how most people find partners, form families, and build lives together — and if that market is distorted by money — then the apps aren't just affecting individuals. They're shaping who mates with whom at a societal scale.

The cascade

Money determines visibility on dating apps. Visibility determines who matches with whom. Matches determine who dates, who mates, who forms families. Families shape communities and society. Therefore: money is influencing the formation of families and society through distorted dating markets.

This isn't hypothetical. It's arithmetic. When wealth gives you a measurable advantage in the system that determines who you partner with, it creates a bias that compounds across millions of people and ripples through generations.

Everyone ends up less happy.

The people who pay feel anxious — “Am I paying enough? Should I upgrade to Platinum?” The people who don't pay feel invisible and frustrated. The gap between the two creates resentment on both sides.

And the platforms themselves? They're structurally incentivized to keep you single. The moment you find love, you cancel your subscription. A happy couple is a churned customer. The entire business model profits from loneliness.

No wonder 79% of Gen Z report dating app fatigue. The system isn't broken by accident. It's broken by design.

This is why HeartFull exists. Not to build a better dating app — but to fix the broken incentive at the root. Disconnect money from connections entirely. Let character determine who you meet, not your credit card.
